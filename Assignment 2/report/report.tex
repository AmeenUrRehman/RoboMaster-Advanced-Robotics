\begin{abstract}\noindent
A RoboMaster EP searches for a floor target (Tag~3), aligns with it, follows a smooth trajectory generated with differential flatness, stops 20\,cm in front of it, and logs the four wheel-motor voltages once per second.
The solution is a ROS~2 package (\texttt{rm\_flatness\_nav}) built from small, tested pieces: a closed-form flatness inversion and a cubic boundary-value solver, a four-state supervisory state machine, an AprilTag detector with pose recovery, a wheel-rate and armature-voltage model, and a ROS~2 node that connects them to the robot.
All hardware results come from real logs on the robot; every terminal figure is rendered from the captured text of the real command, and every plot comes from the real CSV files.
Three complete autonomous runs over USB ended with the state \texttt{ARRIVED}, each with all four voltages exactly $0.000$\,V at the halt and a peak speed of about $0.137$\,m/s (limit $0.16$\,m/s). Run~5, the one used for Table~1 and the plot, used the three printed tags lying flat on the floor as the assignment describes; runs~3 and~4 used the tag standing upright. The stop gap, measured by hand, was 20\,cm in runs 3 and 4 (to the upright tag); in run 5 it was about 20\,cm to the edge of the A4 sheet, which is about 30\,cm to the black square and about 38\,cm to the tag centre (see Section~4), i.e.\ the robot stopped about 18\,cm short of the planned 20\,cm to the tag centre.
The report also documents what did \emph{not} work (two earlier runs that never halted and several flat-tag attempts with a wrong tag scale), what was learned from the camera (it is fixed to the chassis and does not tilt with the arm), and where the setup deviates from the assignment text (an upright tag in runs 3 and 4, a start distance of 1.2 to 1.5\,m instead of 1.5 to 1.8\,m, and a stop that comes from the planned trajectory rather than from a camera reading).
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem 1: Visual search, in-place centering and standoff guard}

\subsection{(i) Downward camera setup}
\paragraph{Why the camera must look down.} The tags lie on the floor, so the useful part of the field of view is the floor. A level camera at height $h$ sees a floor point at distance $d$ at a depression angle $\arctan(h/d)$. With the vertical half field of view of the stream (about $30^\circ$, from $f_y\approx316$\,px at $360$\,p), a level camera loses the floor in front of it once $\arctan(h/d)>30^\circ$, i.e.\ closer than $h/\tan30^\circ$, so the tag would leave the image before the 20\,cm standoff. Tilting the camera down by $\beta$ moves the visible floor band closer, to $h/\tan(30^\circ+\beta)$, and makes the view of the floor less grazing, which reduces the foreshortening of a flat tag.

\paragraph{What was measured.} The driver was commanded to the pose $(X,Y)=(105,30)$\,mm (the driver names the vertical axis $z$) and the arm read back $x=0.105$\,m, $z=0.03$\,m. Camera frames were then captured at this pose and at other arm positions (Figure~\ref{fig:armposes}): the frames \emph{did not change} when only the arm moved. The robot description in TF attaches the camera to the arm, but on this robot the camera is fixed to the chassis, so its tilt is not set by the arm pose, and that part of TF is not used. In the images the horizon (the bases of the far walls) lies roughly 100 to 130\,px above the image centre, which corresponds to a downward tilt of about $20^\circ\pm3^\circ$ (the tilt is read from the images and is an estimate). The gripper claw is visible at the bottom centre of every frame, so a floor tag directly ahead and closer than roughly 0.3\,m is hidden by it. The arm position $(105,30)$\,mm is a low, retracted pose; the ToF beam clearance was checked from the run log: the sensor module was enabled and in the whole run it never returned a reading below its maximum range, so neither the arm nor the gripper was in its beam. The camera height above the floor was not measured.

\paragraph{Why the tag was upright, and how far it is visible.} A flat floor tag seen from a camera about 0.25\,m above the floor is strongly foreshortened: for a 15\,cm tag the model of Figure~\ref{fig:vis} (camera height assumed) gives only 11\,px of height at 1\,m and 5\,px at 1.5\,m, which a detector cannot read. An upright tag keeps its full width: $317\cdot0.15/d$ pixels, i.e.\ 47\,px at 1\,m. The detection limit was measured by shrinking a real frame: the tag was found at 41\,px at native size and not at 33\,px; after enlarging the image twice before detection it was found at 33\,px and 27\,px and not at 24\,px. The detector therefore tries native size first and the doubled image if nothing is found (21\,ms per empty frame). Later tests with flat tags (Section on flat tags) showed that they are nevertheless readable out to about 0.6 to 1\,m, which allowed run 5.

\paragraph{Sign convention.} The bearing $\theta>0$ means the tag is to the \emph{left} of the camera axis (ROS REP-103, $+\omega_z$ counter-clockwise), so aligning is $\omega_z=+K\theta$. In SEARCHING the direction of the sweep is the side on which the tag was last seen, and counter-clockwise if it has never been seen.

\paragraph{Why align in place before translating.} The planned path is a straight cubic in the robot frame (the boundary conditions require $y(0)=y(T)=0$), so it assumes the tag lies straight ahead. If the robot drove while its heading was off by $\theta$, the open-loop path would end laterally displaced by about $d\sin\theta$: for the real run ($d=1.28$\,m) a $10^\circ$ error already gives $1.28\sin10^\circ=22$\,cm, which is larger than the tag itself. The tag would also drift toward the edge of the image (the half field of view is about $45^\circ$), where it is partly clipped or lost. Rotating first removes the heading error at zero translation, so the planned and executed paths coincide and the tag stays near the image centre. Both real runs show this: after the first two seconds of the chase the logged bearing error stayed within 0.02\,rad.

\subsection{(iii) Standoff braking}
The brake is the first test in every control tick, before any other state logic: if the camera ground distance is $d\le0.20$\,m or the front ToF reads $\le0.20$\,m, the state becomes ARRIVED and $(v,\omega)=(0,0)$ is commanded immediately and the state is latched. This behaviour is covered by unit tests (\texttt{test\_fsm\_brake\_on\_camera\_and\_tof}). \textbf{On the robot neither sensor brake was triggered}: in both runs the upright tag left the top of the image at about 0.35 to 0.38\,m, and the ToF never returned a reading below its maximum range, so the stop came from the end of the planned trajectory (state \texttt{ARRIVED (trajectory end, tag out of view)}), which is designed to end exactly at the standoff. The measured gap of 20\,cm confirms that this worked in these two runs.

\paragraph{Video stalls.} The runs used the driver's own decoder and the published topic \texttt{camera/image\_color}. The driver log contains no decoder or buffer errors (its only warning is that the optional FFmpeg transport package is not installed). The optional direct-capture mode of the node (\texttt{camera\_source:=cv2}) implements the recommended grab thread with a \texttt{time.sleep(0.010)} between \texttt{cap.grab()} calls; it was not needed and was not used.

\section{Problem 2: Differential flatness trajectory generation}

\subsection{(i) Flatness inversion}
For a unicycle $\dot x=v\cos\theta$, $\dot y=v\sin\theta$, $\dot\theta=\omega$, the position $z(t)=[x(t),y(t)]^T$ is a flat output: the speed is $v=\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}$ and the heading is $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\dot y,\dot x)$, so
\begin{equation}
v=\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2},\qquad
\omega=\dot\theta=\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{atan2}(\dot y,\dot x)=\frac{\dot x\ddot y-\dot y\ddot x}{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}.\tag{1}
\end{equation}
At standstill ($\dot x^2+\dot y^2\to0$) the heading is undefined; the implementation returns $\omega=0$ there. The formula was verified on a circle of radius $R$ traversed at angular rate $\Omega$ (it returns $v=R\Omega$ and $\omega=\Omega$; unit test \texttt{test\_flat\_inversion\_circle}).

\subsection{(ii) Cubic polynomial matrix solution}
Each component is $z_j(t)=\alpha_0^{[j]}+\alpha_1^{[j]}t+\alpha_2^{[j]}t^2+\alpha_3^{[j]}t^3$ ($j\in\{x,y\}$) over the horizon $T$. Imposing $z_j(0)$, $\dot z_j(0)$, $z_j(T)$, $\dot z_j(T)$ gives the linear system
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&0\\1&T&T^2&T^3\\0&1&2T&3T^2\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_0^{[j]}\\\alpha_1^{[j]}\\\alpha_2^{[j]}\\\alpha_3^{[j]}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}z_j(0)\\\dot z_j(0)\\z_j(T)\\\dot z_j(T)\end{bmatrix},\tag{2}
\end{equation}
which \texttt{solve\_flat\_polynomial(z0, zdot0, zf, zdotf, T)} solves with \texttt{numpy.linalg.solve}. With the boundary values of the assignment, $x(0)=0$, $\dot x(0)=0.06$\,m/s, $x(T)=d_{\rm tag}-0.20$\,m, $\dot x(T)=0$ and $y(0)=\dot y(0)=y(T)=\dot y(T)=0$, the closed form is
\[
\alpha_0=0,\quad \alpha_1=\dot z_0,\quad
\alpha_2=\frac{3z_f-2\dot z_0T}{T^2},\quad
\alpha_3=\frac{\dot z_0T-2z_f}{T^3}\qquad(\dot z_f=0),
\]
(obtained by solving the last two rows of Eq.~(2) for $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_3$). For $y$ all four boundary values are zero, hence $\alpha^{[y]}=\mathbf 0$, $y(t)\equiv0$ and, by Eq.~(1), $\omega(t)\equiv0$: the flat path is a straight line in the robot frame and the speed profile is $v(t)=\dot x(t)=\alpha_1+2\alpha_2t+3\alpha_3t^2$. The turning during the chase comes only from the small bearing correction.

\subsection{(iii) Time horizon scaling}
\begin{equation}
T=\max\!\left(4.0,\ \frac{1.6\,(d_0-0.20)}{v_{\max}}\right),\qquad v_{\max}=0.16\ \text{m/s}.\tag{3}
\end{equation}
The mean speed of the path is $z_f/T=(d_0-0.20)/T=v_{\max}/1.6=0.10$\,m/s, and a cubic that starts slowly and ends at rest peaks at roughly 1.4 times its mean. Because $T$ scales with the distance, the peak is the same for every $d_0$ (the 4\,s floor only matters for starts closer than $0.2+4\cdot0.16/1.6=0.6$\,m). The coefficient table below was computed with the package; the peak is 0.137\,m/s in all cases, below 0.16\,m/s, and $v(T)=0$. In the real runs, $d_0=1.37$\,m (run 3) and 1.28\,m (run 4) give $T=11.7$\,s and 10.8\,s, and the chases lasted 11.8\,s and 10.9\,s (the extra 0.1 to 0.2\,s is the control-tick granularity).

\section{Problem 3: Motor voltage telemetry and dynamics logging}

\subsection{(i) Kinematics to wheel rates}
With $r=0.05$\,m and $L_c=0.20$\,m, Eq.~(4) of the assignment is implemented as
\begin{equation}
\omega_1=\omega_4=\frac{v_x+L_c\omega_z}{r},\qquad \omega_2=\omega_3=\frac{v_x-L_c\omega_z}{r}\tag{4}
\end{equation}
(wheel~1 is front-right, 2 front-left, 3 rear-left, 4 rear-right). Numerical examples that appear in the logs: the in-place search at $\omega_z=0.30$\,rad/s gives $\omega=\pm1.2$\,rad/s; the peak cruising speed $v_x=0.137$\,m/s gives $\omega=2.74$\,rad/s ($=26.2$\,rpm) on all four wheels.

\subsection{(ii) Motor armature voltage model}
\begin{equation}
V_i(t)=K_v\,\omega_i(t)+K_a\,\frac{\omega_i(t)-\omega_i(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t},\qquad K_v=0.38\ \mathrm{V\,s/rad},\ \ K_a=0.06\ \mathrm{V\,s^2/rad}.\tag{5}
\end{equation}
It is evaluated in every control tick ($\Delta t=0.05$\,s) on the wheel rates computed from the \emph{commanded} $(v_x,\omega_z)$. The voltages are therefore model outputs: the motor controller's own voltage is not measured. The first tick uses a zero acceleration term, and once the robot has halted the wheel rates are zero for two consecutive ticks, so $V_i=0$ exactly (no acceleration term left). Figure~\ref{fig:vterms} splits the logged $V_1$ of run 4 into its two terms: the back-EMF term $K_v\omega_1$ carries the whole cruise (up to $1.05$\,V), and the inertia term $K_a\Delta\omega_1/\Delta t$ is nonzero only at the velocity steps (starting to turn in SEARCHING, the switch from ALIGNING to CHASING, where the speed jumps to the initial 0.06\,m/s, and the final deceleration), reaching about $+1.4$\,V at the start of the chase and $-1.3$\,V at an in-place reversal.

\section{Run 5 and the trials with the printed tags lying flat}
After the runs above, all three printed tags (\texttt{tag36h11}, IDs 1, 2, 3, black square 150\,mm) were laid flat on the carpet, as the assignment describes, with Tag~3 in the middle and Tags~1 and~2 about 0.3\,m to its left and right as distractors. The node follows only ID~3.
\paragraph{Static detection test (real camera frames).} With the row 0.6\,m in front of the robot, the project's own detector (native scale) found all three tags: Tag~1 83$\times$27\,px, Tag~3 69$\times$27\,px, Tag~2 84$\times$28\,px (Figure~\ref{fig:flat}). With the row moved to about 0.8\,m only Tag~2 was found, at 47$\times$9\,px, and Tag~3 was missed; at about 1\,m nothing was found. So in a single frame a flat tag is readable out to roughly 0.6 to 0.7\,m with this camera. The node's search is more tolerant (it also tries a 2$\times$ enlarged image and sees the tag over several frames).

\paragraph{Mission attempts.} Four autonomous attempts were made with this layout. \textbf{One reached ARRIVED: run 5} (Table~1 and the main plot). In the first attempt the tag was not detected during a 33\,s sweep. The other two (later) attempts detected the tag, aligned and chased for several seconds, then lost it and returned to SEARCHING without halting; in both the logged distances were about 100 times too large (for example 95.8 in the log, i.e.\ 0.96\,m if the factor of 100 is removed), which is what a tag size typed as 15 instead of 0.15 would produce; because of this scale error the rule that continues the trajectory when the tag leaves the image (it only applies within 1.8\,m) could not act. Run 5 has no 20\,Hz log (only \texttt{motor\_voltage\_log.csv} at 1\,Hz was written), so its plot has one point per second.

\section{Deviations from the assignment text and limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tag orientation.} The assignment places the tag \emph{flat} on the floor. Run 5 does so. Runs 3 and 4 used the tag standing upright because a flat 15\,cm tag is a thin sliver for this camera (Figure~\ref{fig:vis}); a single frame reads a flat tag only out to about 0.6 to 0.7\,m, although the node (with its upscaled search and several frames) found it at 1.07\,m in run 5.
  \item \textbf{Distance.} The assignment gives 1.5 to 1.8\,m. In run 5 the tags started about 1.2 to 1.5\,m away by hand measurement (the camera reported 1.07\,m to the tag centre at the first detection), at or slightly below that range, and the robot stopped about 20\,cm from the sheet edge (about 38\,cm from the tag centre) instead of the planned 20\,cm. In runs 3 and 4: The tag was about 1.3 to 1.4\,m away (the robot only rotates before the chase, so the camera range at the first detection is the starting distance: 1.375\,m in run 3 and 1.28\,m in run 4). The detector could see further with its upscaled search, but the tag was not placed there.
  \item \textbf{Camera and arm.} The arm pose did not change the camera view; the camera tilt of about $20^\circ$ was read from images.
  \item \textbf{Stop source.} The stop came from the planned trajectory, not from a camera or ToF reading. The sensor brakes are implemented and unit-tested but were not triggered on the robot.
  \item \textbf{Voltages} are model values computed from commanded velocities (Eq.~(5)); they are not measured at the motors.
  \item \textbf{Number of trials.} Three successful runs (3 and 4 upright, 5 flat); stop gaps were measured by hand to about 1\,cm. Run 5 has only a 1\,Hz log and a hand-measured start range.
  \item \textbf{Tags~1 and~2.} Runs 3 and~4 used Tag~3 alone. In run~5 and the other flat-tag trials, Tags~1 and~2 lay beside Tag~3 as distractors; the node followed only ID~3 and the log does not record which other tags were seen.
\end{itemize}
